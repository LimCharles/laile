# Recovering from a hip replacement

This is general information about the usual recovery after a total hip replacement. Your surgeon and physiotherapist set your own precautions, targets and timeline. Follow their advice over this guide.

## Hip precautions

After some hip replacements, especially when the surgeon operated from the back of the hip (the posterior approach), you may be asked to avoid, for a set time (often around 6 weeks, sometimes longer):
- bending your hip past a right angle (90°), for example sitting in low chairs or bending down to pick things up;
- crossing your legs or ankles;
- twisting the operated leg inwards.

After other approaches (for example the anterior approach), the precautions can be different, or fewer.

**Only your surgeon's instructions apply to you.** Laile stores your clinician's limits, such as a maximum hip bend, and blocks any exercise that would go past them.

## Everyday tips your team may give you

- Use a firm, higher chair with armrests, and a raised toilet seat if needed.
- Use a long-handled reacher and sock aid instead of bending down.
- Keep a pillow between your knees when lying on your side, if advised.
- Getting in and out of a car and bed has special techniques. Your physiotherapist will show you.

## Exercises commonly used after a hip replacement

Your program lists the ones you do. In Laile's library, these can include:
- ankle pumps (circulation);
- quad sets (thigh);
- heel slides, only as far as your hip precautions allow;
- glute bridges (buttocks), when your team says so;
- straight-leg raises, if prescribed;
- sit-to-stand from a suitably high chair;
- mini squats while holding support.

## A typical timeline

- **First 2 weeks:** Walk with your aid. Do ankle pumps often. Gentle strengthening. Follow your precautions.
- **Weeks 2 to 6:** Walk further. Build strength in the buttock and thigh muscles. Many people move from a frame to a stick.
- **Weeks 6 to 12:** Precautions are often reviewed by the surgeon. Balance, stairs and longer walks. Return to more activities.
- **3 months and beyond:** steady improvement for up to a year.

## Signs to report

- A hip that suddenly becomes very painful, with the leg looking shorter or turned. This can be a dislocation: stop, keep still, and get urgent help.
- A swollen, hot or painful calf; a red, hot or leaking wound; or a fever. See the red flags guide.
