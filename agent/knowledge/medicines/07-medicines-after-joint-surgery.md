# Medicines commonly used after joint surgery

This guide explains what these medicines are for and what to look out for. It is not advice on whether to take them, how much, or when.

- **Always follow the label and the instructions from your doctor or pharmacist.**
- **Questions about doses, timing, missed doses, or stopping a medicine: ask your doctor or pharmacist.**

If a medicine is not listed here, Lele will say: "I'm not able to identify that medication, please check with your pharmacist."

## Pain relief

### Paracetamol (also called acetaminophen; brand names include Panadol)
- **For:** pain and fever. It is often taken regularly for the first weeks after surgery, as part of a pain plan.
- **Good to know:** Many other products, such as cold and flu remedies, also contain paracetamol. Taking two at once can mean too much. Never take more than the label or your doctor says in a day. Too much can seriously damage the liver.
- **Side effects:** uncommon at normal doses.

### Anti-inflammatory painkillers (NSAIDs)
- **Examples:** ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac, celecoxib, etoricoxib (brand name Arcoxia).
- **For:** pain and inflammation (swelling).
- **Good to know:** They can irritate the stomach, so they are usually taken with food, and sometimes prescribed with a stomach-protecting medicine. They can affect the kidneys and raise blood pressure. They can add to bleeding risk when taken with blood thinners, so your doctor needs to know everything you take.
- **Get help urgently:** black or tarry stools, vomiting blood, or severe stomach pain.
- **Anti-inflammatory gels** (for example diclofenac gel) are rubbed on the skin. Don't use them on broken skin or the surgical wound.

### Stronger painkillers (opioids)
- **Examples:** tramadol, oxycodone, codeine (sometimes combined with paracetamol).
- **For:** stronger pain, usually for a short time after surgery.
- **Common effects:** Drowsiness and dizziness, which raise the chance of a fall. Stand up slowly and use your walking aid. Constipation. A laxative is often prescribed alongside. Nausea.
- **Good to know:** Don't drink alcohol, and don't drive while taking them. They are usually reduced and stopped as pain improves. Your doctor plans this.
- **Get help urgently:** someone who is very hard to wake, or whose breathing is very slow.

### Nerve pain medicines
- **Examples:** pregabalin, gabapentin.
- **For:** nerve-type pain (burning, shooting, tingling).
- **Common effects:** dizziness and drowsiness.
- **Good to know:** don't stop suddenly without advice.

## Preventing blood clots

After a hip or knee replacement, the risk of a clot in the leg (deep vein thrombosis) or lungs is higher for a while. Medicines that lower this risk are often given for a set period, commonly a few weeks.

- **Examples:** enoxaparin (an injection under the skin, often into the belly); rivaroxaban, apixaban and dabigatran (tablets); aspirin (used in some hospitals' plans).
- **Common effects:** bruising more easily; bleeding gums or nosebleeds.
- **Get help:** blood in urine, black stools, vomiting blood, a severe headache, or bleeding that won't stop.
- **Good to know:** Don't stop early, or take extra, unless your doctor says so. If you miss a dose, ask your pharmacist what to do. Tell any dentist or doctor that you're on a blood thinner.
- **Exercise helps too:** ankle pumps and walking keep blood moving. They work alongside the medicine; they don't replace it.

## Other medicines you may be given

- **Laxatives or stool softeners** (for example lactulose, senna, docusate): for constipation, which is common with opioids and less movement. Fluids and walking help too.
- **Stomach protectors** (for example omeprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole): protect the stomach, often alongside anti-inflammatories.
- **Anti-sickness medicines** (for example ondansetron, metoclopramide): for nausea.

## Pain relief and exercise

- Being comfortable enough to exercise matters for recovery. Some care teams plan pain relief around exercise sessions. Whether that applies to you, and when to take anything, is a question for your doctor, pharmacist or physiotherapist, not Lele.
- If pain is stopping you exercising, tell your clinician; your pain plan may need a review.

## Questions to ask your doctor or pharmacist

- What is each medicine for, and how long will I take it?
- What should I do if I miss a dose?
- Which side effects should I watch for?
- Can I take it with my other medicines and supplements?
